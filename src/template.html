Data and sources

Sources (accessed 16 Oct 2026)

  • BYU/NASA Scatterometer Climate Record Pathfinder, Antarctic Iceberg Tracking Database, consolidated database v8.0, one CSV per iceberg (consolidated_database_v8.0.zip, files dated 8 May 2026). Primary source.
  • U.S. National Ice Center, Antarctic iceberg weekly CSV archive: 615 weekly files, 7 Nov 2014 to 15 Oct 2026. The file for 28 Aug 2020 returned HTTP 404. Used after a berg's BYU record ends, for bergs BYU does not have, and for reported areas.
  • Natural Earth 1:50m land and 1:50m Antarctic ice shelves, v5.1.2, clipped south of 20°S. Ice shelves are drawn in a lighter tint than grounded ice.

How the tracks were built

  • Every berg with a NIC name (A, B, C or D, a number, and fragment letters) in either source. BYU's 267 unnamed scatterometer tracks (sa, e, uk files) are left out.
  • BYU's README defines a per-sensor flag: 1 for an observation, 0 for a day BYU interpolated. Only observations are used ( interpolated rows dropped).
  • When several sensors report the same day, the scatterometer fix is used (ASCAT, QuikSCAT, OSCAT, SeaWinds, NSCAT, ERS, SASS in that order), and the NIC fix only on days without one. BYU notes that some NIC reports are inconsistent; several have stale positions or a flipped longitude sign.
  • Nothing is interpolated. A marker sits at its latest fix; a line joins two fixes only when they are at most 10 days apart. Before the 1990s NIC reported most bergs weeks or months apart, so early tracks are mostly dots.
  • Area: the USNIC reported area where given (from Apr 2024); otherwise an estimate from NIC length and width in nautical miles, treating the berg as an ellipse (π/4 × L × W); for the ten bergs on BYU's all-time size ranking, that maximum size before their first NIC dimensions. Markers keep the latest reported size. Size shows area only: it drops when a berg breaks up or its edges waste away, but thinning, which makes up much of the mass loss, is not in these sources, so mass is not shown.
  • Distance travelled is a running estimate: straight lines between successive fixes, counted only between fixes from the same sensor (NIC and the scatterometers place a berg's centre up to tens of km apart) and only once the berg has moved more than 10 km from the last counted point. Spikes are skipped and jumps add nothing. Each fragment starts at 0. It is rough: position noise still adds about 45 km a year to a stationary berg (the grounded A23a, 1992–2019), while sensor switches and gaps drop some real distance; other reasonable rules give totals up to 25% lower.

Quality control

  • fixes north of 45°S are flagged but drawn, since they belong to consistent tracks (). The northernmost is . The map reaches about 44°S on its axes to keep them in frame.
  • spikes: fixes that disagree by more than 100 km/day with most fixes within ±5 days, or short excursions the track returns from. They stay in the CSV, flagged, and are hidden from the tracks.
  • jumps: consecutive fixes more than 100 km/day apart that are not isolated spikes. The line breaks there.
  • NIC reused the name A23B: BYU's A23B is a 1991–1999 piece of A23, USNIC's A23B a 2025 piece of A23A. Both sit under one name with a 25-year gap.
  • A23a has no fix after 31 Mar 2026 and is absent from both current lists (USNIC 15 Oct 2026, BYU 9 Oct 2026).
